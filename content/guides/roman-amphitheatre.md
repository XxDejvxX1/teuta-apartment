Most people come to Durrës for the water and leave without knowing that one of
the largest Roman amphitheatres in the Balkans is sitting a few streets back
from the port, under the houses.

It was built in the second century, when the town was called Dyrrachium and the
road to Constantinople started at its harbour. Then it was abandoned, filled in,
and forgotten thoroughly enough that people spent generations building homes on
top of it without knowing. It was found again in the 1960s. You can still see
where the excavation stopped: the far side of the bowl runs straight into the
back walls of houses that are still lived in, with washing out.

## What there is to see

The seating is stone and largely intact on the excavated side, and you can walk
down into the arena floor. It is quieter than it should be for something this
size — usually a few people, a lot of grass, and cats.

The part I would not skip is the small Byzantine chapel built into one of the
lower passages, centuries after the arena stopped being used for anything. It
has a wall mosaic inside. It is a strange thing to come across in the corridor
of a Roman theatre, which is exactly why it stays with you.

## Making a morning of it

The amphitheatre is close enough to the Byzantine walls and the Venetian tower
by the port that all three are one walk rather than three trips. The
archaeological museum is on the seafront on the way back, so you can start
inland and finish looking at the water.

Go early. There is very little shade in the bowl and by midday in July it holds
the heat like a pan.

## Getting there from the apartment

It is a walk of about fifteen minutes along the front and then inland, or five
minutes in a taxi. Anyone in town will point you at it if you ask for the
amfiteatri.
